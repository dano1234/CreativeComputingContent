## **Get Electrons to Do Your Bidding**

I have never seen electrons but believing in them is very useful. Basically they are simple creatures they want to get from one place to another. If you put some work in between those places, they will do it to get past. They want to get from one side of the battery to the other.  For this week we are using the Arduino as the power supply so they are trying to get from the 3.3V pin to the ground pin. (STAY AWAY FROM THE ELECTRICITY COMING DIRECTLY OUT OF THE WALL FOR NOW!) We are going to use a wire as a road for them to make this journey. But the trick is we are going to place a light, a LED, in the way for the electrons to light along the way.

  

  

## **The Dreaded Short Circuit**

These electrons are not completely benevolent. If you give them a free ride from one place to another they get too excited bouncing off the walls of the wire and bad things happen like your house will burn down or at least your wire and battery might get very hot. Make them think they can get to the other side but make sure you use them up before they get to the other side. Add useless work for them (a resistor) if you have to just to slow them down.

  

  

## **Knowing more about Electrons**

So far you are probably imagining electrons as horny little scrubbing bubbles (does anyone have such an image) desperate to get to the other side of the wire. People study this in a great deal more depth but the truth is you don’t have to go to much further before it is all pretty mysterious even to physicists. In digital electronics, the electricity is reduced to a yes or a no and we use software for more nuanced behavior. Without software, you would need a much greater understanding of electricity to coax the electrons through a more elaborate obstacle course of chutes and ladders called analog circuits. Even if for digital circuits you should know 4 things.

  

  

## **Things I Hope you Learn With Cliff Analogy**

*   Positive is like an invisible cliff and ground is like the ground at the bottom of the cliff.
    
*   Voltage is how High the cliff is. (Measured in Volts)
    
*   Amperage is how many seats on the bus about to go off the cliff. (Measured in Amps)
    
*   Resistance is how many shrubberies on the side of the cliff slowing the fall of the bus. (Measured in Ohms)
    
*   Power is how bad the accident will be at the bottom. (Measured in Watts)
    

  

## **Stuff You are Supposed to Know But You Probably Won’t Use In This Class**

Most of the time we will be reading, not writing circuit diagrams so we don’t really need to know the theory behind them. [Ohm’s Law (Resistance=Volts/Amps) is super famous](http://vimeo.com/76442432) but you may never need it. It does come in handy in making sure your circuit is not close to the dreaded short circuit. For instance, if the LED is not enough work for those electrons they will make trouble. In that case, you have to add a little “make work” in the form of a resistor to tire out the electrons.  Most of the time there is a diagram (they call it a schematic) that tells you what amount of resistor to put in there so you don’t have to calculate it yourself.

Another formula Watts = Volts\*Amps is useful for finding out how big a power supply to get. Occasionally you need to know how electricity behaves in parallel and in series. [Using a multimeter](http://itp.nyu.edu/physcomp/Labs/Electronics) might be important for debugging things like are you really getting 5V from the places you think you should be.

![](https://itp.nyu.edu/classes/cc-f25-dano/wp-content/uploads/sites/372/2024/09/Screenshot-2024-09-03-at-11.54.16%E2%80%AFAM.png)

The “AC” power coming out of the wall is big power that can actually move physical things or heat them up. Our task is really in the world of information where we just need enough electricity to make a signal.  In our world 5V or 3.3V (our Arduino is 3.3) is the signal for yes (aka 1,true,on, high, positive) and 0 Volts is a no (aka 0,false,off,low,negative). “AC” means alternating current which means that is switches direction many times every which would make a constant signal kind of complicated. So we are going to use “DC” or direct current. [You can use a “wall wort” power supply to convert from AC to DC](http://www.radioshack.com/product/index.jsp?productId=21779276&znt_campaign=Category_CMS&znt_source=CAT&znt_medium=RSCOM&znt_content=CT2818119). Or you can get it directly out of a battery which is naturally “DC” power. [Some people use voltage regulators.](http://itp.nyu.edu/physcomp/Labs/Breadboard) We know you are eventually going to be using a microcontroller (Arduino Brand) which can provide the perfect amount of power for your projects. It pulls the power from your laptop via USB and then has place for you to get +5V and Ground. You might consider detaching it from your computer while you fumble around with the wires on your breadboard and reattaching it once you think you have the wires settled.

## **Resistor**

![](https://itp.nyu.edu/classes/cc-f25-dano/wp-content/uploads/sites/372/2024/09/Screenshot-2024-09-03-at-11.54.49%E2%80%AFAM.png)

![](https://itp.nyu.edu/classes/cc-f25-dano/wp-content/uploads/sites/372/2024/09/Screenshot-2024-09-03-at-11.59.32%E2%80%AFAM.png)

Resistors resist the flow of electrical current. They reduce the voltage and limit the current. The bands on a resistor indicate the resistor’s value. Here’s a handy [resistor color code calculator](http://www.dannyg.com/examples/res2/resistor.htm). For our LED’s you can probably add a 220 ohm (Red Red Brown) or 570 ohms (Green Blue Brown) that will eat up the electricity not used by the LED.

  

## **LED**

![](https://itp.nyu.edu/classes/cc-f25-dano/wp-content/uploads/sites/372/2024/09/red-led-pinout-schematic.jpeg)

Diodes are used as one-way streets in circuits. In our circuit we are using a Light Emitting Diode (LED) which lights up when it performs it’s job. We don’t really care about the usual diode job of policing flow but really just the fact that it lights up. The key thing to know about a diode is that it is polarized, meaning that the two legs are not interchangeable. On most LED’s the longer lead (anode, triangle) goes closer to +5 and the shorter leg (cathode, line) goes closer to ground. LED’s may not eat up most of your 5V and risk looking like the dreaded short circuit without an added resistor. [Less than a 1000 ohm, say 220 ohms should slow down the electrons enough](http://vimeo.com/78674965). If it does not light or is too dim, try without.  [If you don’t use any resistor the LED will probably just burn out faster but the superbrights may explode](http://vimeo.com/78966835).

  

## **Breadboard**

![](https://itp.nyu.edu/classes/cc-f25-dano/wp-content/uploads/sites/372/2024/09/Screenshot-2024-09-03-at-12.11.06%E2%80%AFPM.png)

These allow you to connect wires without soldering. The holes in a row are invisibly connected underneath so just put wires in the same row to connect them. (rows are not connected across the center) will be connected.  Why the long rows on the sides? Because our main trick is putting work for electrons to do in their journey from positive to negative those will be the most popular destinations on your breadboard and we dedicate the long row on the side for them. Only solid wire will work well with these holes if you like to use more flexible stranded wire for long runs to your sensors you have to put “headers” at the end that will fit in the holes. [Here is much more about the breadboard.](https://itp.nyu.edu/physcomp/labs/labs-electronics/breadboard/)

## **Soldering**

![](https://itp.nyu.edu/classes/cc-f25-dano/wp-content/uploads/sites/372/2024/09/Screenshot-2024-09-03-at-12.11.54%E2%80%AFPM.png)

To make good connections you have to use the soldering iron to heat the two things you are joining and then touch the solder to the joint not directly to the soldering iron. If you are impatient you will be tempted to just touch the solder directly to the soldering iron. Your patience will be less tested if the tip of the soldering iron is clean and quickly conducting heat to the joint. You can clean the tip by melting a bunch of solder on it and wiping with the wet sponge. The really hard thing about soldering is that there are four things to hold (two pieces of the joint, the iron and the solder) and you only have two hands. You might use a clamp of some sort to help. Also, don’t breath in the fumes. [Here is a video](https://vimeo.com/107049478). [More pictures](https://itp.nyu.edu/physcomp/Labs/Soldering).
